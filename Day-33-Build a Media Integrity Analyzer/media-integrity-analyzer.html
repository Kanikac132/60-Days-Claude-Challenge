Why this matters

🧭 You read hundreds of headlines a week

Every one of them was written by someone who wanted you to click, feel, share, or believe something. That's not automatically bad — but it means the words in front of you were chosen, not neutral. Media literacy is the habit of noticing that choice.

Challenge 1 of 2

Headline Detective

The concept

🕵️ Headlines are sales pitches for articles

A headline's job is to earn a click — sometimes at the expense of accuracy. Writers stretch verbs ("slams," "destroys"), imply certainty that doesn't exist ("proves," "confirms"), or drop context that would make a claim feel smaller.

Everyday use: before reacting to or sharing a headline, ask "does the article actually support this exact claim?" Most misinformation isn't a lie — it's a headline overselling a much smaller story.

    Emotion Detector

    The concept

    ❤️‍🔥 Feelings travel faster than facts

    Content that makes you feel something strong — awe, fear, outrage, urgency — gets shared more than neutral content. Creators and advertisers know this, so posts are often built around one target emotion, using specific word choices to trigger it.

    Everyday use: when a post makes you feel a strong emotion before you've even processed the facts, that's worth a pause. The stronger the feeling, the more worth double-checking the claim.
